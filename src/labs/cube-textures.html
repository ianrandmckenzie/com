The_Big_Question

How does one person with big, lofty ideas reasonably translate that to practical execution with a reasonable timeline?

Say what you want about AI (there is regrettably, a lot), the one thing most developers can agree on is that we can now shift our focus less on writing lines of logic and more on being code reviewers for lines created by AI. For a project like making a video game, this means that someone like me can focus more on story, art, and most importantly, game design mechanics that are genuinely fun and exhilirating.

Yet, with 3 distinct sky nations all with their own visual themes, compromises must be made even with things like art. For this reason, I have chosen to divide my artwork in a few ways:

  1. 01Character attachments (such as tools and weapons): Modelled, textured, and rigged by me
  2. 02Complex environment objects (like grass swaying in the wind): Modelled, textured, and rigged by me
  3. 03Entities (such as livestock and creatures) and character models: Modelled, textured, and rigged by me
The_Big_Problem

I accumulated a lot of experience with this using Blockbench, a voxel-game focused 3d modeling app, when I did contract work making rigged 3d models and textures for people making custom mods and servers for Hytale, a game created by the hosts of the most widely known and played independent server for Minecraft. Yet, this leaves a big, glaring hole. The voxels - cubes - for the environment. In these early stages, there are already over 200 textures needed in the production pipeline. This requirement will easily balloon to over 2000 as I progress in development.

A to-do list of that size is simply not feasible, particularly for someone like me spending a big chunk of each day managing my schizophrenia disability. So, the immediate answer is: let's use AI. Yet, if you know anything about making an atlas for meshing onto a 3d model, you know that such a task can be confusing for even for humans. To try and make a bizarre looking image and have that translate to something passable looking after mapping onto a 3d object is inevitably going to be a frustrating endeavour to get AI to do. This is compounded to an impossible degree at scale (that 2000+ number I mentioned earlier).

This bottleneck is one of the reasons I paused my work for a while when I first started boilerplating the voxel engine at the beginning of this year. But, at some point, something clicked in my head. Sure, AI sucks at making an atlas-conforming texture. But, they are EXCELLENT at outputting text. They are excellent at code. Why not just have it output a JSON instead of an actual image?

The_JSON_Answer

Instead of prompting AI to generate an image, I can instead prompt AI to simply generate a JSON object based on a handful of preset definitions. We then paste that into a preview tool, iterate if/as needed, then export the png texture atlas from the previewer itself. Instead of spending 30 to 90 minutes on each texture, I can now spend 2 to 10 minutes instead. Multiple that by 2,000 and we now have a workflow that is realistic for a solo developer.

The_Slop_Caveat

The logical question anyone who may consider themselves an artist might be, "aren't I just making more AI slop?" Maybe I am. But, with my limitations, I think pragmatism is a better answer. I like to think that one day I will go back and redo all the textures by hand. Whether or not that happens depends entirely on whether or not I sell this game and if it makes enough money to be a feasible thing to do. Until then, I am content with a balanced approach that prioritizes story and fun, with only complex and animated objects being hand-crafted by me (which will still be an enormous amount).
