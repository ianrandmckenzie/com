An argument in favor of Long-duration Crowd Control

Category Game Design Theory
Topic PvP Combat Systems
Focus Long-duration Crowd Control
Text Human-written
First Published Nov. 19, 2026
Long_Duration_Crowd_Control

First, we need to define what I mean by long-duration crowd control. When I say long-duration, there are four things to keep in mind about how it functions:

  1. 01It is not a stun effect in the traditional sense because taking damage immediately breaks the effect
  2. 02In some games, there are no diminishing returns; instead, there is a long-duration immunity to the effect
  3. 03It has historically taken shape through "sleep," "polymorph," "mesmerize," or "mind control" animations and flavor text, where the victim is stopped in their tracks and unable to take action
  4. 04Alternatively, it can come in the form of a "root" effect where they are held in place physically, but can still perform spellcasting actions like healing or offensive spells, or ranged attacks with a bow, crossbow, or with throwing weapons

Long-duration crowd control was often used in legacy MMORPGs like Lineage 2, EverQuest, and a game I've sunk over 10,000 hours into: Dark Age of Camelot (DAoC). This form of crowd control is seldom used in contemporary video game design. Arguing against long-duration crowd control, a game designer might tell you that it is bad game design because it's not fun. Being taken out of the fight for 30 seconds or more, twiddling your thumbs while watching all your friends around you die is indeed a miserable prospect.

Going by that premise at face value, I'm inclined to agree that it's not fun, if you're not interested in competitive gameplay. Yet, if I am playing a video game and have opted myself into player-versus-player conflict, isn't competition exactly what I'm looking for? Anyone who is being honest with themselves about PvP knows that we aren't sitting down to get our asses handed to us because we want to put on some lo-fi hip-hop, have a soothing cup of tea, and chill out with some low-impact gaming.

Raising_the_stakes

Being taken out of the fight for 30 seconds or more at a time raises the stakes of every fight. It demands situational awareness at all times. It requires constant panning of your camera to watch for incoming threats. It demands coordination with your group members: listening for callouts announcing "incoming!" and bursting out of formation so that you don't all get mesmerized in a centralized cluster.

The fact that area-of-effect (AoE) long-duration crowd control can empower a group of 8 people to feasibly wipe out a group of 40+ other players in a single fight isn't the unbalanced or unfair mechanism people may have you believe. It is instead a tactical advantage that can be utilized to the benefit of smaller, more coordinated, and more ambitious groups. It forces larger numbers not to rest on the laurels of manpower alone. It can even arguably make combat more realistic: superior numbers do not guarantee victory when coordination and tactics are decisive.

The_Forgotten_Archetype

In most cooperative RPGs, characters can be distilled into three archetypes: healer (heal damage), DPS (deal damage), and tank (soak damage).

In more traditional contemporary PvP systems, combat is largely reduced to an obsessive mastering of cooldown rotation with opportunistic bursts of large damage during coordinated stuns/stun-locks. Sure, it's perhaps more engaging if we go by a metric of actions per minute. But to me, all this system does is replace cognitive friction with ergonomic friction. Players are forced to speedrun carpal tunnel syndrome in order to become top ranked arena battlers, all while being deprived of a compelling and fun Controller archetype of RPG gameplay.

The controller archetype is often a counter to itself in PvP settings, with the ability to cure other group members who are mesmerized. I'm not sure if I've ever seen a game where a character was built to fill the controller role completely; games typically make controllers a hybrid role with secondary skills like healing or damage dealing.

Closing_Remarks

If you played with the simulator above, you may have noticed the zerg often prevails. This is especially true when you ramp up their ability to effectively use countermeasures. The more you tip the scales in a balance of skill, you can see that long-duration crowd control tends to be a non-factor. It becomes a pillar of tactical necessity. If you are slow to use or counter it, you do so at your own peril.

These results also further one of the key points I need to stress: long-duration crowd control is only imbalanced if a game designer makes it imbalanced. In the early days of Dark Age of Camelot, it absolutely was overpowered. But as the DAoC devs gradually introduced abilities like purge, a castable cure mesmerize, and other countermeasures, long-duration crowd control became like many abilities — extremely strong if not addressed promptly through skill, preventative tactics, and reaction time.
